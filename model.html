What the score means#

The impact score is an estimate of the probability that a paper ends up in the top 10 % most-cited papers of its field for its publication year. It uses only information available on the day the paper appears, so it works for papers that have no citations yet.

What it looks at#

Four broad kinds of information feed the score: the venue, the authors' recent track record, basic facts about the paper, and how fast its topic is growing. The score does not read the paper's text.

How well it works#

The model was trained on papers from one year and tested on papers from the following year, so every test paper was unseen during training.

Among the tenth of test papers it ranked highest, 93 % really did reach the top 10 %. The gain over journal rank is largest outside the most selective journals, where most highly cited papers in the test year were published.

Missing scores and estimates#

  • Reviews get no score, because reviews are cited for different reasons than research papers.
  • Preprints are still chosen with the version of the model that ignores the venue, but their impact meter shows something simpler: the share of recent arXiv preprints in the same topics whose first and last authors have fewer citations over the past three years. It appears as a dashed meter and is not a probability. No meter appears when the last author's publication record cannot be found.
  • Unfamiliar journals get an estimate from a version of the model that ignores the venue. It appears as a dashed meter marked "est." and is less reliable.

Limits#

  • The score ranks papers. It does not judge their quality, and a low score is not a reason to skip a paper.
  • It was validated on one pair of years and on papers from established journals, so small differences between papers are noise.
  • Citations measure attention, not correctness.
